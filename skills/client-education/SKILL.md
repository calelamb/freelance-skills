# Client education — explain so they can decide

An explanation the client cannot read is a formality: they disengage or override you. The goal is not to sound authoritative or to make anyone feel good — it is an accurate picture in their vocabulary plus **exactly one decision they can make**. Unread or bundled, the choice comes back to you.

## Procedure

### 1. Separate reporting from asking
Sort everything into **facts**, **risks**, and **decisions** (what someone must choose). Count the decisions. Four means one skimmed reply.

### 2. Translate before you narrate
Lead with the trade-off in business outcomes — money, dates, customers, reputation — mechanism only if asked. Every technical term gets an analogy or gets cut: "the card can charge twice, like two staff ringing up the same customer." Naming a mechanism then adding "don't worry about the details" is explaining *at* someone. Speed-vs-safety in plain form: shipping now buys the date and spends the certainty.

### 3. State the downside in their stakes
A message with no downside is marketing, not information. Name what breaks, who sees it, when: "if it fails, it fails live in front of the board, with no rollback mid-meeting."

### 4. Ask one decision, park the rest
Surface the one choice blocking the next hour of work, with two or three options and your recommendation. Every other decision gets a line — "separate question, coming back to you." Never bundle timing, scope, risk, and money.

### 5. Route decisions that are not the client's
Money, hours, rates, and what counts as billable belong to the freelancer, never to you. Extra work you spot goes to `scope-check`, then `quote-job` if it proceeds; never into "current scope" as a favor. Billing goes to `invoice-prep` and the freelancer first.

### 6. Get explicit sign-off on risk shortcuts
Bypassing staging or skipping `deploy-verify` is a decision the client makes in writing — their own reply in a durable channel (chat, email, ticket) naming the shortcut and its consequence. A yes on a call counts only once you post "confirming: <shortcut>, meaning <consequence> if it fails" and they answer. Your recap alone is not sign-off; silence is not consent. Progress with no decision in it is a `status-update`, not this skill.

### 7. Pressure changes the wording, never the content
Rules 5 and 6 hold whoever is pushing — the client's deadline, or the freelancer's own "just handle it," "don't lose this client." Fatigue is not authorization: it never licenses deciding a shortcut for the client, settling a billing question, or dropping a risk line to warm the tone. Ask the one question back; keep the downside in.

## Output template
```
What's happening: <one sentence, no jargon>
Why it's slower: <analogy, not mechanism>
Trade-off: A <outcome + risk> · B <outcome + risk>
Recommendation: <one option, one line why>
What I need from you: <ONE question, answerable yes/no or A/B>
Parked: <other decisions, one line each — not asked today>
```

## Red flags — STOP, they cannot act on this
- It asks the client to decide more than one thing
- A term they'd have to search, followed by "don't worry about it"
- Mechanism given, never translated into money, dates, or customers
- No downside anywhere — nothing saying what failure looks like to them
- A "skip staging" yes with the consequence unstated, or a verbal/assumed yes standing in for sign-off
- You settled a billing, hours, or rate question instead of asking the freelancer
- Extra work — upgrade, cleanup, migration — folded into "current scope" as a favor
- You optimized for "keep them happy" over an accurate picture
- "Just get it deployed" read as authority to decide process, scope, or billing too
- A risk line softened or cut because someone was frustrated or tired

## Failure modes this prevents
- An unreadable or bundled message, so the client disengages or decides nothing
- A risky shortcut taken silently, then blamed on the freelancer when it fails live
- Money or scope calls made on the freelancer's behalf, discovered at invoicing
- A reassured client who later feels misled because the downside was never stated
