# Late payment follow-up — the schedule sends the email, not your mood

The **second message is the one that never gets sent** — every one after the first costs social capital, so it is postponed into never. Escalation runs on a calendar, not feelings: each stage fires at its offset, one step firmer, however the last conversation felt. Warmth is not payment.

## Procedure

### 1. Establish the clock before drafting
Record due date, days overdue, amount, terms (net-N, late fee, pause rights), and the date and stage of the last message. Compute the stage due **now**; if offsets have passed, jump straight to it — never restart at friendly, never more than 14 days between stages.

**A jump skips tone, not substance.** Every consequence not yet in writing — work pause, late fee — folds into that one message with its own date; none takes effect in the message that first announces it.

**Never invent a term.** No fee, percentage, deadline, or pause right enters a draft unless quoted from the contract. Not in hand? Ask for the clause; a draft written first carries `[late fee — clause __]` and is not sent.

### 2. Send the stage that is due — tone only steps up
- **Day 7 — friendly.** Invoice re-attached, one ask, one named date. No apology for asking.
- **Day 21 — firm.** Terms quoted verbatim, the next consequence, its calendar date. The stage that gets skipped, and the one that works.
- **Day 35 — work-pause notice.** When work stops, what stops, what stays safe (their data, their access). Sent **before** pausing.
- **Day 45 — formal notice.** Contracted late fee applied, marked final, deadline stated, reissued via `invoice-prep`.

### 3. Keep money and relationship in separate messages — send both
Never bundle the ask with good news, deliverables, or a new phase. Praise or project news gets its own two-sentence reply — same day, separate channel, no payment content, no commitment to future work until the balance clears. A next phase is priced in `quote-job`, scoped in `scope-check`; progress goes in `status-update`; terms tighten via `client-onboarding`.

## Output template
```
Stage due: <day 7/21/35/45> · Invoice <#> · $<amt> · <N> days overdue · terms: <net-N>
Last contact: <date>, stage <day> · Next fires: <date>
Jumped stages: <n> → consequences carried in: <pause / fee>
Terms source: <clause, quoted> | NOT IN HAND — placeholder, blocks send

Subject: Invoice <#> — <status word>
<the clause that now applies, quoted>
<each consequence and its date>
Ask: payment by <date>, or a written date they commit to.

Reassurance: <what, when> → in writing? <y/n>
Relationship reply: separate <y/n>
```

## Red flags — STOP, the sequence is stalling
- "The check's coming" treated as a reason to wait — reassurance **advances** the sequence (restate it in writing, with a date), never pauses it
- Future work is dangled and the invoice feels less urgent — the bias this schedule counteracts
- Your draft is softer than the last one sent — escalation moves one direction
- "Let it ride" accepted without naming the cost: bills due now, no consequence set for late payment
- The ask shares a message with project excitement or a deliverable
- "I'll check back in a couple of weeks" instead of a dated stage
- 45+ days overdue, late fees and payment terms still unmentioned
- A fee or deadline you cannot quote from the contract
- A jumped stage's notice left unsent — the pause never announced
- You feel awkward and are about to not send this — that is the failure mode, happening now

## Failure modes this prevents
- The second email never sent, because the first felt awkward enough
- The sequence resetting to friendly whenever reassurance arrives
- Late-fee terms never invoked — or invented ones never on paper
